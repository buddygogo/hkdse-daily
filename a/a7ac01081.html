Hong Kong recorded its hottest day on recordthe highest temperature ever measured since records began有紀錄以來最熱的一天 this summer, as the Observatory headquarters registered 36.9 degrees Celsius while Typhoon Dolphin swept warm air across the region. The reading, the highest since records began in 1884, was eclipsed in some districts: Sheung Shui in the New Territories logged 39.8 degrees, the highest figure recorded since automatic weather stations were introduced in the 1980s. The extreme heat forced many residents to seek refuge in shopping malls and even artificial snow parks, while outdoor workers and domestic helpers were reported to have struggled significantly under the scorching conditionsextremely hot weather conditions酷熱的天氣.

It is widely argued that such records are no longer isolated anomalies but part of a discernible patterna pattern that can be clearly noticed or recognised明顯的規律 of intensifying heat linked to climate change. Meteorologists have noted that Hong Kong is projected to face several typhoons annually in the coming years, and that rising sea and air temperatures are likely to make such record-breaking days increasingly common rather than exceptional. Critics contend that current public health measures, including heat warnings and outdoor work guidelines, have not kept pace with the frequency and severity of these events, leaving vulnerable groups disproportionately exposedaffected to an unfairly large degree compared with others承受不成比例的影響 to heat-related risks.

By the same token, environmental groups argue that record heat should serve as a wake-up callan event that makes people realise a serious problem exists警號 for accelerating the city's transition towards carbon neutrality, rather than being treated merely as a short-term inconvenience. Nevertheless, sceptics point out that individual weather records, however striking, cannot alone justify sweeping policy change without sustained long-term data. What seems increasingly clear is that as extreme heat becomes a more regular feature of Hong Kong summers, pressure will continue to mount on policymakers to reassess how the city prepares its infrastructure, workforce and public health systems for a warmer future.
