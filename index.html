Mainland Investors Pivot to Hong Kong AI Stocks, Pulling Back From Financials

South China Morning Post ·3 September 2026

It is widely reported that mainland Chinese investors have been reallocating capitalmoving money from one investment to another重新分配資金 away from traditional financial stocks and towards artificial intelligence firms listed in Hong Kong. Southbound funds channelled a record HK$10.6 billion into MiniMax, an AI start-up, during August alone, an inflow that eclipsedsurpassed; overshadowed超越；蓋過 even the sums directed towards established giants such as Alibaba and Tencent. Since becoming eligible for trading through Stock Connect on 6 August, MiniMax has seen mainland investors accumulate a 9.7 per cent stake, with shares surging as much as 22.8 per cent on their first day of southbound trading.

Critics contend that such a concentrated bet on a single, relatively young sector is not without risk. Nevertheless, proponents argue that the enthusiasm reflects a broader paradigm shifta fundamental change in approach範式轉移 in how mainland capital views Hong Kong's stock market: no longer merely a venue for banks and property developers, but increasingly a gatewaya means of access or entry門戶；途徑 to cutting-edge technology. MiniMax and its peer Zhipu AI, both listed in January 2026, rallied by more than 300 per cent by June and were subsequently admitted to the Hang Seng Tech Index, a milestone that cementedmade firm or secure鞏固 their status among the city's most closely watched equities.

By the same token, analysts caution that this AI-driven rally could prove volatileliable to change rapidly and unpredictably反覆無常的；動蕩的 once initial lock-up periods expire and early investors seek to realise profits. It is nonetheless undeniable that Hong Kong's role as a hub for AI listings has been significantly strengthened, offering a timely case study for students examining how technological innovation and capital markets are inextricably linkedconnected in a way that cannot be separated密不可分地聯繫 in the modern economy.

CityU Orientation Camp Scandal Deepens as Bruised Students' Photos Surface

The Standard ·3 September 2026

It is widely argued that university orientation camps, while intended to foster camaraderiebuild friendship and mutual trust培養情誼 among new students, have in recent years become associated with troubling practices. This controversy resurfaced this week at the City University of Hong Kong, where fresh photographs surfacedcame to public attention浮現；曝光 showing male and female students with visible bruising on their thighs and red marks across their backs. The images circulated widely on social media only days after an earlier video, depicting a shirtless male student carrying a woman dressed in red underwear, had already gone viral and drawn public criticism.

Critics contend that such initiation rituals amount to a form of hazinghumiliating or dangerous initiation activities迎新迫害；欺凌式迎新 that normalises physical harm under the guise of tradition. In response, the university has established a disciplinary investigation committee, which is currently interviewing both the camp's organisers and its participants in order to establish accountability. The student union committee responsible for staging the event, known by the name “Messianis”, has since announced its own dissolution, a move widely seen as an attempt to contain the falloutlimit the negative consequences of an incident控制事件的後果 from the scandal.

Nevertheless, commentators point out that dissolving a single committee does little to address the underlying culture that permits such practices to persist year after year. By the same token, it is argued that universities must move beyond reactive damage control and instead implement robust, preventive safeguards — including clear codes of conduct and independent monitoring — if genuine reform is to be achieved. The episode has reignited a broader debate about student welfare, peer pressure, and the balance between tradition and safety on Hong Kong's university campuses.

Pet Dogs Could Be Reclassified as Fighting Breeds During Routine Checks

RTHK ·3 September 2026

It is widely reported that pet owners across Hong Kong may find their dogs reclassifiedplaced into a different official category重新分類 as fighting breeds during otherwise routine licensing and vaccination appointments. According to the Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation Department, this measure follows a marked upticka small increase輕微上升 in public reports concerning suspected fighting dogs, prompting officers to be deployed more frequently for on-site inspections. Principal veterinary officer Michelle Yeung explained that identification currently relies on visual assessment, whereby veterinarians evaluate a dog's head shape, stature and overall body structure to determine its breed category.

Critics contend that such subjective, appearance-based assessments risk producing inconsistent outcomesresults that vary or contradict each other不一致的結果, particularly for mixed-breed dogs whose ancestry cannot be determined at a glance. Nevertheless, officials point out that the system offers a practical opportunity for oversight: since dog licences must be renewed every three years alongside a rabies booster vaccination, staff can observe each animal at that time and revise its classification if necessary. Owners of dogs confirmed as fighting breeds are not required to surrender their pets, provided they comply with all relevant restrictions and safety requirements.

By the same token, this policy tightening comes in the wake of a fatal attack last month in Ta Kwu Ling, in which a woman running a licensed dog shelter was mauled to death by an animal in her care, an incident that prompted the government to pledge a review of the legal definition of “fighting dogs” and to submit legislative amendments within the year. The episode illustrates how a single tragedy can galvaniseshock into action激勵；促使採取行動 swift regulatory change, while also raising important questions about public safety, animal welfare and the limits of visual identification methods.

Health Authorities Find No Sign of WT1 Jab in Unregistered Vaccine Raids

RTHK ·3 September 2026

It is widely reported that Hong Kong's health authorities have concluded a major operation targeting the sale of unregistered medical injections, following mounting public concern over clinics allegedly offering an unproven cancer-prevention jab known as WT1. Assistant Director of Health Frank Chan confirmed that, despite raids on fourteen premises resulting in three arrests and the seizure of more than 890 vaccine vials, investigators found no evidence that any WT1 vaccine had actually been administered, nor did they identify a single patient who had received one.

Nevertheless, the operation was far from fruitless: among the seized items were roughly twenty types of unregistered injectionsmedical products not officially approved for use未經註冊的注射劑, the majority of which turned out to be vitamin and amino acid preparations rather than genuine vaccines. Critics contend that the very existence of such an underground market reflects a troubling willingness among some consumers to seek out unverified treatments, often lured byattracted or tempted by被……吸引 exaggerated health claims circulating on social media.

By the same token, Mr Chan issued a stark warning that individuals who obtain unregistered WT1 injections could be placing their lives at risk, citing the danger of bacterial contaminationinfection caused by harmful bacteria細菌污染 during improper manufacturing or administration. It is nonetheless undeniable that this episode serves as a cautionary talea story that warns against a course of action警世故事 for the wider public: consumers are strongly urged to verify that any medical product has been properly registered before consenting to receive it, rather than relying on unsubstantiated promises of miraculous health benefits.

🧋 'Spritea' Craze: Hongkongers Are Dunking Tea Bags in Sprite

Full analysis▾

A quirky drink hack has taken Hong Kong by storm: creators are dunkingdipping something briefly into liquid浸泡 ordinary tea bags directly into bottles of Sprite, producing a sweet, malty, citrus-laced concoction that has been dubbed “Spritea”. Having already gone viral in North America in 2025, the trend has now taken offbecome suddenly very popular迅速流行起來 among Hong Kong's Gen Z social media users, with searches for the recipe surging by more than 200 per cent on TikTok within a single week. Sensing an opportunity, Sprite has since launched an official ready-to-drink version and toured a mobile pop-up tea shop through high-traffic districts, giving away tens of thousands of free samples.

  • Psychological appeal: The novelty and sheer simplicity of the DIY hack — just add a tea bag to a soda bottle — makes it easy and satisfying to replicate, encouraging repeat filming and sharing.
  • Social media influence: Short-form video creators amplified the trend rapidly, turning a quirky kitchen experiment into a viral challenge that spread across TikTok and Instagram within days.
  • Consumer culture: Brands capitalised swiftly on organic online buzz, with Sprite's official product launch and sampling campaign further fuelling demand and cementing the trend commercially.
