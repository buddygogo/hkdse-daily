Hong Kong's Tai Hang Fire Dragon Returns to Light Up Mid-Autumn Festival

Hong Kong Free Press ·15 September 2026

It is widely argued that few events capture the intangible cultural heritagea tradition or practice, rather than a physical object, passed down through generations非物質文化遺產 of Hong Kong quite as vividly as the Tai Hang Fire Dragon Dance. Returning this week from 24 to 26 September to coincide with the Mid-Autumn Festival on 25 September, the dance traces its origins back to the 1880s, when villagers first performed the ritual in an effort to ward offto prevent something harmful from affecting you驅除 a plague believed to be haunting the community. Nearly a century and a half later, the tradition endures as one of the city’s most treasured cultural spectacles, formally inscribed on the National List of Intangible Cultural Heritage.

This year’s performance will, as always, feature a colossalextremely large in size or extent龐大的 67-metre dragon studded with more than 12,000 smouldering incense sticks, borne aloft by close to 300 performers as it winds through the narrow lanes of Wun Sha Street and Tung Lo Wan Road in Causeway Bay. Nevertheless, the celebrations are by no means confined to Tai Hang alone: on the evening of the festival itself, the dragon will process to Victoria Park, where it joins an inaugural international lantern spectacular featuring elaborate Qinhuai lanterns imported from Nanjing. By the same token, this fusion of neighbourhood ritual and citywide festivity illustrates how deeply Mid-Autumn traditions remain woven intoclosely combined with something, forming an essential part of it融入 the rhythm of Hong Kong life.

Critics contend that, amid rapid urban redevelopment, such traditions are increasingly vulnerable to erosion, making sustained community stewardshipthe careful and responsible management of something entrusted to your care悉心保育 all the more essential. It is nonetheless heartening that the Tai Hang Residents’ Welfare Association continues to pass the craft of dragon-making down to younger generations, ensuring that this raucousmaking a loud, disorderly noise喧鬧的 , incense-scented spectacle will continue to light up the autumn sky for years to come.

Hong Kong Police Warn That AI Poses a Growing National Security Threat

South China Morning Post ·22 September 2026

It is widely acknowledged that artificial intelligence has become a double-edged swordsomething that has both beneficial and harmful effects雙刃劍 for governments across the world, and Hong Kong is no exception. Speaking ahead of his retirement, outgoing deputy police commissioner Andrew Kan Kai-yan cautionedto warn someone about a possible danger警告 that AI now poses an emerging threat to national security, even as the force increasingly turns to the same technology to help combat crime. His remarks echo concerns voiced by state security minister Chen Yixin, who has called for a more robuststrong and effective, not easily broken or defeated穩健的 risk-prevention framework and stronger global governance over the technology.

Critics contend that the rapid pace of AI development has outstripped the ability of regulators to keep up, leaving authorities in a perpetual game of catch-up. Nevertheless, it would be an oversimplification to view AI purely as a liability: the police force itself has begun to harnessto control and use the power of something運用 AI tools to detect fraud patterns and analyse large volumes of data, suggesting that the same technology capable of amplifying threats may also be instrumental in countering them.

By the same token, this dual-useable to serve both civilian and security or military purposes兩用的 dilemma is far from unique to Hong Kong; it reflects a wider anxiety shared by policymakers worldwide as they grapple with how to regulate a technology evolving faster than the laws designed to govern it. It is therefore argued that a coordinated, cross-border approach to AI governance will be indispensable if such risks are to be effectively contained, rather than merely acknowledged.

Hong Kong 'Lacks a Climate Narrative' to Unite Its Green Efforts, Critics Say

South China Morning Post ·23 September 2026

It has often been observed that Hong Kong possesses no shortage of environmental targets, yet critics contend that the city still lacks a unifying climate narrativea clear, shared story or vision that connects separate policies into one coherent goal缺乏統一的氣候論述 capable of rallying the public behind its green ambitions. Under the newly unveiled first five-year plan, the government has set out twenty-two major indicators, five of which are binding targetsgoals that must legally or officially be met具約束力的指標 concentrated largely on environmental protection, including reductions in carbon emissions per unit of GDP and an increased share of zero-carbon electricity generation.

Nevertheless, observers argue that isolated statistics, however ambitious, do little to galvaniseto shock or excite someone into action激勵 public engagement unless they are woven into a coherent story that ordinary citizens can understand and support. The administration has reaffirmed its long-standing pledge to cut total carbon emissions by 50 per cent before 2035, relative to 2005 levels, en route to achieving carbon neutralitya state in which the amount of carbon released is balanced by an equivalent amount removed碳中和 before 2050, chiefly through energy conservation, the promotion of green transport, and reduction of waste. By the same token, such commitments will only translate into tangible progress if implementation is accompanied by sustained public communication.

It is nonetheless argued that Hong Kong’s climate policy need not start from scratch; rather, what appears to be missing is a compelling framework capable of connecting disparate initiativesseparate and different efforts that do not obviously belong together各自為政的措施 , from waste levies to green financing, into a single, comprehensible vision. Should such a narrative fail to materialise, critics warn, ambitious targets risk remaining abstract figures on a policy document rather than a shared civic mission.

Hong Kong Schools Told to Build AI Literacy Under New Digital Education Blueprint

The Standard ·17 June 2026

It is widely recognised that artificial intelligence is reshapingto change the form or nature of something重塑 classrooms around the world, and Hong Kong schools are now being asked to catch up in earnest. Under the Education Bureau’s newly released Blueprint for Digital Education Development, all publicly funded schools must integrateto combine one thing with another so they work as a whole融入 digital education into their school development plans for the 2026-27 academic year, alongside a fresh AI literacy learning framework built around three guiding principles: learning AI well, using it effectively, and using it responsibly.

To support this transition, the bureau has disbursed a one-off grant of $500,000 to every publicly funded school under a three-year funding programme aimed at empowering learning and teaching through AI. Teachers, meanwhile, will be required to complete no fewer than 30 hours of digital education training within each three-year professional development cycle, with the bureau pledging to offer some 50,000 training places annually. Nevertheless, some educators have voiced reservationsdoubts or concerns about a plan or idea保留意見 about whether such training can be delivered at scale without overburdening already stretched teaching staff.

By the same token, proponents argue that equipping the next generation with genuine AI literacy is no longer optional but essential, given how pervasiveexisting or spreading widely throughout a place or group無處不在的 the technology has already become in daily life. Whether this ambitious blueprint succeeds will depend, in no small part, on how effectively schools translate policy directives into meaningful classroom practice.

🥤 Sprite + Lemon Tea: Hong Kong's Viral DIY Drink Mashup

Social Trend

A new DIY drink craze has been fizzing across Hong Kong social media this September, as teenagers mix and matchto combine different things in various ways隨意配搭 supermarket staples to create their own customisedmade or changed to suit a particular person's needs度身訂造的 beverages. The most talked-about combination pairs Sprite with classic Hong Kong-style iced lemon tea, producing a fizzy, citrusy hybrid that has spread rapidly through short recipe clips. Creators film themselves pouring, taste-testing and rating each attempt, turning a simple drink into shareable content. It is widely argued that the trend reflects a broader appetite among young people for playful self-expression through everyday consumption, one video at a time.

HKDSE Writing Angles
  • Psychological appeal: The novelty of an unexpected flavour pairing triggers curiosity and a small dopamine reward each time viewers watch someone taste-test a new mix.
  • Social media influence: Short-form video platforms reward easily replicable content, letting the trend spread rapidly as viewers film their own versions and tag friends.
  • Consumer culture: Cheap, widely available ingredients make the trend low-cost and low-risk, encouraging experimentation and repeat participation among budget-conscious teenagers.
